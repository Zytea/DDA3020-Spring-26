## 2.1 SVM derivation, soft margin, and kernel SVM

### (1) Large-margin derivation

For a hyperplane

$$
f_{w,b}(x)=w^\top x+b=0,
$$

the distance from $x_i$ to the hyperplane is

$$
\frac{|w^\top x_i+b|}{\|w\|}.
$$

For correctly separated data, $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)>0$. Because scaling $(w,b)$ does not change the hyperplane, choose the canonical scale

$$
\min_i y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1.
$$

Then the closest points have distance $1/\|w\|$ from the boundary, so the margin width is $2/\|w\|$. Maximizing the margin is equivalent to minimizing $\frac12\|w\|^2$ subject to

$$
y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge 1.
$$

### (2) Hinge-loss derivation

The hinge loss is

$$
\ell_i=\max\{0,1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\}.
$$

The regularized hinge-loss objective is

$$
C\sum_{i=1}^n\max\{0,1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\}+\frac12\|w\|^2.
$$

In the hard-margin separable case, zero hinge loss for every sample requires

$$
1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\le0,
$$

which is exactly

$$
y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1.
$$

Thus the constrained hard-margin SVM objective is recovered.

### (3) Lagrangian, KKT, dual, w, b, and support vectors

The solution of $w,b$ is symbolic because no concrete training data are given. We first derive the dual problem to obtain $\alpha^*$, then recover $w^*$ and $b^*$ from the KKT conditions.

Write the constraints as

$$
1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\le0.
$$

The Lagrangian is

$$
L(w,b,\alpha)=\frac12\|w\|^2+
\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i(1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)),
\qquad \alpha_i\ge0.
$$

Stationarity gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=0
\Longrightarrow
w=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i x_i,
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=0
\Longrightarrow
\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0.
$$

The KKT conditions are

$$
y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1,\qquad
\alpha_i\ge0,\qquad
\alpha_i(1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b))=0.
$$

Substitute the stationarity conditions into the Lagrangian. Since
$w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(w,b,\alpha)
&=\frac12\|w\|^2+\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i
-\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i w^\top x_i
-b\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i\\
&=\frac12\|w\|^2+\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-w^\top w\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\|w\|^2\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the dual problem is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_\alpha\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0,\qquad \alpha_i\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $\alpha^*$ be the optimal dual solution. Then

$$
w^*=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i^* y_i x_i.
$$

For any support vector with $0<\alpha_i^*$, the hard-margin condition gives

$$
y_i({w^*}^\top x_i+b^*)=1,
$$

so

$$
b^*=y_i-{w^*}^\top x_i
$$

for such a support vector, or averaged over support vectors for numerical stability.

A training sample is a support vector when $\alpha_i^*>0$. By complementary slackness, such samples lie on the margin and directly affect the decision boundary.

### (4) Soft-margin SVM with slack variables

For non-separable or overlapping data, the hard-margin constraints may be infeasible. Introduce slack variables $\xi_i\ge0$ so that each sample is allowed to violate the unit-margin constraint:

$$
y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
1-\xi_i-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\le0,\qquad -\xi_i\le0.
$$

The soft-margin primal problem is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{w,b,\xi}\quad
&\frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&1-\xi_i-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\le0,\qquad i=1,\dots,n,\\
&-\xi_i\le0,\qquad i=1,\dots,n.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $C$ controls the tradeoff between margin size and training violations. Larger $C$ penalizes violations more heavily and behaves closer to hard-margin SVM; smaller $C$ allows more violations.

Introduce Lagrange multipliers $\alpha_i\ge0$ for
$1-\xi_i-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\le0$ and $\mu_i\ge0$ for $-\xi_i\le0$. The Lagrangian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(w,b,\xi,\alpha,\mu)
&=\frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i\\
&\quad+\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i\left(1-\xi_i-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\right)
-\sum_{i=1}^n\mu_i\xi_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

Stationarity gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=0
\Longrightarrow
w=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i x_i,
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=0
\Longrightarrow
\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0,
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \xi_i}=0
\Longrightarrow
C-\alpha_i-\mu_i=0.
$$

Since $\alpha_i\ge0$ and $\mu_i\ge0$, the last equation implies

$$
0\le\alpha_i\le C.
$$

The KKT conditions are

$$
y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\qquad \xi_i\ge0,
$$

$$
\alpha_i\ge0,\qquad \mu_i\ge0,
$$

$$
\alpha_i\left(1-\xi_i-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\right)=0,\qquad
\mu_i\xi_i=0.
$$

Substitute the stationarity conditions into the Lagrangian. Because
$C-\alpha_i-\mu_i=0$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
L
&=\frac12\|w\|^2+\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i
-\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i w^\top x_i
-b\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i
+\sum_{i=1}^n(C-\alpha_i-\mu_i)\xi_i\\
&=\frac12\|w\|^2+\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-w^\top w\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\|w\|^2\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the soft-margin dual problem is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_\alpha\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0,\\
&0\le\alpha_i\le C,\qquad i=1,\dots,n.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the soft-margin dual has the same objective as the hard-margin dual, but the constraint changes from $\alpha_i\ge0$ to $0\le\alpha_i\le C$.

The slack variable also has a direct geometric meaning:

- $\xi_i=0$: correctly classified and on/outside the margin.
- $0<\xi_i<1$: correctly classified but inside the margin.
- $\xi_i=1$: exactly on the decision boundary.
- $\xi_i>1$: misclassified.

For interpreting $\alpha_i$:

- $\alpha_i=0$: usually outside the margin and not a support vector.
- $0<\alpha_i<C$: on the margin boundary, with $\xi_i=0$; these points are useful for computing $b$.
- $\alpha_i=C$: margin-violating support vector; it may be inside the margin or misclassified.

For any point with $0<\alpha_j<C$, complementary slackness gives

$$
y_j(w^\top x_j+b)=1,
$$

so

$$
b=y_j-\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i x_i^\top x_j.
$$

### (5) Kernel SVM

The reason kernels work is visible from the dual problem: data appear only through inner products $x_i^\top x_j$. For nonlinear classification, map the input into a feature space

$$
x\mapsto \phi(x),
$$

and run a linear SVM there. The soft-margin primal problem in feature space is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{w,b,\xi}\quad
&\frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&y_i(w^\top\phi(x_i)+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\\
&\xi_i\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Repeating the same Lagrange derivation gives

$$
w=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i),\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0,\qquad
0\le\alpha_i\le C.
$$

After substitution, the dual becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_\alpha\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j \phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j)\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0,\\
&0\le\alpha_i\le C.
\end{aligned}
$$

Define the kernel function

$$
k(x_i,x_j)=\phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j).
$$

Then we never need to compute $\phi(x)$ explicitly. We only replace every inner product with a kernel value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_\alpha\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j k(x_i,x_j)\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i=0,\\
&0\le\alpha_i\le C.
\end{aligned}
$$

For hard-margin kernel SVM, replace the box constraint $0\le\alpha_i\le C$ by $\alpha_i\ge0$.

The prediction function is

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x)
&=w^\top\phi(x)+b\\
&=\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)\right)^\top\phi(x)+b\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i k(x_i,x)+b.
\end{aligned}
$$

The classifier is

$$
\hat y=\operatorname{sign}(f(x)).
$$

For any margin support vector with $0<\alpha_j<C$,

$$
b=y_j-\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i y_i k(x_i,x_j),
$$

or average this expression over all such support vectors for numerical stability.

Common kernels include

$$
k(x,z)=(x^\top z+c)^d
\qquad\text{(polynomial kernel),}
$$

$$
k(x,z)=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-z\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
=\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2)
\qquad\text{(RBF/Gaussian kernel),}
$$

and

$$
k(x,z)=\tanh(\kappa x^\top z+c)
\qquad\text{(sigmoid kernel).}
$$

Only samples with $\alpha_i>0$ appear in the final decision function, so kernel SVM is still expressed through support vectors.

## 2.6 PCA derivation

### (1) Centered reconstruction

For general mean $\mu$, the low-dimensional code is

$$
z_n=U^\top(x^{(n)}-\mu).
$$

The reconstruction is

$$
\tilde{x}^{(n)}=Uz_n+\mu=UU^\top(x^{(n)}-\mu)+\mu.
$$

Thus $UU^\top$ is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the subspace spanned by the columns of $U$. If $\mu=0$, this reduces to $\tilde{x}^{(n)}=UU^\top x^{(n)}$.

The mean of the reconstructed data is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\mu
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\tilde x^{(n)}\\
&=\mu+UU^\top\left(\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N(x^{(n)}-\mu)\right)\\
&=\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

### (2) Two equivalent formulations

Maximum variance of the reconstructed data:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{U^\top U=I}\quad J_{\mathrm{var}}(U)
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\|\tilde x^{(n)}-\tilde\mu\|^2\\
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\|\tilde x^{(n)}-\mu\|^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\tilde\mu=\mu$.

Minimum reconstruction error:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{U^\top U=I}\quad J_{\mathrm{rec}}(U)
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N
\|x^{(n)}-\tilde{x}^{(n)}\|^2\\
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N
\|(I-UU^\top)(x^{(n)}-\mu)\|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

### (3) Equivalence proof

For each centered vector $x^{(n)}-\mu$, the reconstruction residual is orthogonal to the PCA subspace:

$$
U^\top(x^{(n)}-\tilde{x}^{(n)})=0.
$$

Therefore, by the Pythagorean theorem,

$$
\|x^{(n)}-\mu\|^2
=\|\tilde{x}^{(n)}-\mu\|^2
+\|x^{(n)}-\tilde{x}^{(n)}\|^2.
$$

Averaging over all samples gives

$$
\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\|x^{(n)}-\mu\|^2
=
\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\|\tilde{x}^{(n)}-\mu\|^2
+
\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\|x^{(n)}-\tilde{x}^{(n)}\|^2.
$$

The left-hand side is constant with respect to $U$. Hence maximizing reconstructed-data variance is equivalent to minimizing reconstruction error.

### (4) Obtain U from the covariance matrix

The empirical covariance matrix is

$$
\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N
(x^{(n)}-\mu)(x^{(n)}-\mu)^\top.
$$

The variance objective can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_{\mathrm{var}}(U)
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\|\tilde{x}^{(n)}-\mu\|^2\\
&=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N
(x^{(n)}-\mu)^\top UU^\top(x^{(n)}-\mu)\\
&=\mathrm{Tr}(U^\top\Sigma U).
\end{aligned}
$$

Solve

$$
\max_U\quad \mathrm{Tr}(U^\top\Sigma U)
\qquad
\text{s.t.}\quad U^\top U=I.
$$

Use the matrix Lagrangian

$$
\mathcal L(U,\Lambda)
=\mathrm{Tr}(U^\top\Sigma U)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Lambda^\top(I-U^\top U)\right),
$$

where $\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_K)$. Stationarity gives

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial U}
=2\Sigma U-2U\Lambda=0,
$$

so

$$
\Sigma U=U\Lambda.
$$

Column by column,

$$
\Sigma u_k=\lambda_k u_k,\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.
$$

Thus each $u_k$ is an eigenvector of $\Sigma$. Substituting the eigenvector solution into the objective gives

$$
\mathrm{Tr}(U^\top\Sigma U)
=\sum_{k=1}^K u_k^\top\Sigma u_k
=\sum_{k=1}^K\lambda_k.
$$

To maximize the objective, choose the eigenvectors corresponding to the largest $K$ eigenvalues:

$$
\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_D,
\qquad
U=[q_1,\ldots,q_K].
$$

### (5) PCA algorithm and storage complexity

PPT algorithm:

1. Compute the mean $\mu$ and empirical covariance matrix $\Sigma$.
2. Do eigenvalue decomposition or SVD of $\Sigma$.
3. Sort eigenvectors by eigenvalues from large to small.
4. Pick the top $K$ eigenvectors to form $U=[q_1,\ldots,q_K]\in R^{D\times K}$.
5. For each sample, compute the low-dimensional code $z_n=U^\top(x^{(n)}-\mu)\in R^K$.

After dimensionality reduction, the reduced dataset is

$$
Z=[z_1,\ldots,z_N],\qquad z_n\in R^K.
$$

Thus the minimum storage for the reduced data matrix is

$$
O(NK),
$$

or $O(K)$ per sample. This is the answer if the question only asks for the storage complexity of the dimension-reduced data.

If we also need to project new samples or reconstruct all samples back to the original space, then we must additionally store the PCA basis $U\in R^{D\times K}$ and the mean $\mu\in R^D$. In that case the total storage is

$$
O(NK+DK+D).
$$

Compared with storing the original full data matrix, whose size is $O(ND)$, PCA reduces the sample representation from $D$ numbers to $K$ numbers. The clean exam answer is therefore:

$$
\boxed{O(NK)\text{ for all reduced samples, or }O(K)\text{ per sample}.}
$$
